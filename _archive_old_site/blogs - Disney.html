January 2025 – Present

Walt Disney Imagineering:
Making the Impossible Possible

From PM intern at Disneyland to Associate Project Manager on Tokyo Disney Resort — the role I once thought was out of reach.

I never thought I'd get to start a story with this sentence: I work for Walt Disney Imagineering.

Let me back up.

The path to Imagineering isn't a straight line for most people. Mine wound through a hyperloop team, two rounds of Tesla internships, multiple research labs, a study abroad in Denmark, and three months working as a Systems Engineering intern at Woven by Toyota in Tokyo. Every one of those experiences gave me something: a technical skill, a leadership lesson, an understanding of how high-functioning cross-functional teams actually operate under pressure.

When the offer came to join Imagineering as a Project Management intern in January 2025, I had to sit with it for a second. This is the place that builds the magic — the actual attractions, themed environments, and immersive experiences that make people cry or scream or laugh in ways they don't anywhere else. These projects are built by teams of engineers, creative directors, architects, show designers, and construction managers, all working toward a single vision that has to be exactly right before it opens to guests.

I was going to help coordinate that. The answer was yes before I finished reading the offer.

Disneyland and Disney California Adventure

My first nine months were based out of Glendale, California, working on projects for Disneyland Park and Disney California Adventure. And from day one, it was clear that themed entertainment project management operates at a different frequency than anything I'd encountered before.

The complexity isn't just technical — it's human. You're managing timelines and budgets across teams where each discipline has its own vocabulary, its own risk tolerance, its own definition of "done." Creative teams think in narrative and emotional experience. Engineering teams think in tolerances and load calculations. Construction teams think in sequencing and site constraints. Operations teams think in maintenance and guest flow. A project manager at Imagineering has to speak all of those languages well enough to help each team hear the others.

That's the job. And I loved it.

I supported cross-functional project teams through budget tracking, schedule management, and milestone coordination. I learned what it looks like when a creative direction shift has downstream effects on a fabrication timeline — and how to flag that early enough to give the team real options instead of a crisis. I ran status updates and prepared documentation that needed to be clear across a room full of people from a dozen different departments.

The culture at Imagineering is something I wasn't fully prepared for, in the best way. These are people who care deeply — sometimes impossibly deeply — about the work. They push back when something doesn't feel right. They iterate until it's good. They hold themselves to a standard that starts with "what would a guest remember about this moment?" and works backward from there. It reminded me of the best parts of Tesla — that specific atmosphere of being surrounded by people who genuinely want to get it right, not just done.

The Transfer: Tokyo Portfolio

In October 2025, I was asked to transfer to the Tokyo Portfolio team — working on projects for Tokyo Disney Resort, which includes Tokyo Disneyland and Tokyo DisneySea.

When I found out, I thought about the summer of 2024. I had just arrived in Tokyo as a Woven by Toyota intern, barely speaking any Japanese, navigating a city I'd never been to before, presenting engineering work to executives in a language I'd been learning for two months. I came home from that summer thinking Tokyo had changed me permanently.

I was right — just not in the way I expected.

Fifteen months after landing at Narita Airport as a nervous new intern, I was back working on Tokyo theme park projects. A full circle I did not see coming, and one that still doesn't feel entirely real when I think about it for too long.

The Tokyo Portfolio brought its own set of challenges. International project coordination means managing across time zones, different regulatory frameworks, and communication norms that require even more precision and intentionality than domestic work. Everything I'd learned about cross-cultural communication in Japan — the importance of explicit documentation, visual clarity, assuming less shared context — became directly applicable every single day.

February 2026: The Call

In February 2026, HR confirmed my conversion to full-time Associate Project Manager.

I took a deep breath. A real one. The kind that lasts a few seconds longer than normal.

I called my mom.

She always said life has a way of connecting the dots in reverse — that you can't always see how the pieces fit until you're far enough along to look back. The hyperloop team. Tesla. Denmark. Woven by Toyota in Tokyo. This website, which I built after my first coding class because I just wanted to keep learning. All of it led here.

"I went from a kid who built LEGO sets in Houston to an Associate Project Manager at Walt Disney Imagineering, working on projects for Tokyo Disneyland and DisneySea. My mom was right. The dots do connect."

What This Role Has Taught Me

Project management at a place like Imagineering has a way of making every previous professional experience feel like preparation. The budget discipline from Guadaloop. The manufacturing process thinking from Tesla. The cross-cultural precision from Woven by Toyota. The systems mindset from my ChemE training. All of it shows up in this work in ways I didn't plan for but deeply appreciate.

A few things this role has reinforced:

  • Clarity is an act of respect. When you're coordinating across creative, engineering, construction, and operations — in multiple countries — clear communication isn't just efficient, it's essential. Vague language costs people time they don't have.
  • Relationships are the infrastructure. Every milestone you hit, every schedule you recover, every risk you flag early enough to actually do something about — it happens through relationships. The technical tools are just tools. The people make things happen.
  • The standard is the guest experience. Everything traces back to that. When you're working on a project at a theme park, you're ultimately working on a memory someone is going to carry for the rest of their life. That's a real thing to hold onto when the work gets hard.

The work continues. The Tokyo Portfolio projects are complex, rewarding, and never boring. My goal, same as always, is to leave every project better than I found it — and to keep learning from everyone around me who knows something I don't.

The impossible has a habit of becoming possible if you stay in the room long enough. I plan to keep staying.
